PDF versus CDF: two views of the same distribution

The density tells us where probability is concentrated. The distribution function tells us how much has accumulated.

By Dr. Vishwa Prakash Jha18 August 20267 min read

On this page

Local densityAccumulated probabilityThe connectionThe common mistake

A probability density function and a cumulative distribution function describe the same continuous random variable, but they answer different questions. Confusion disappears once we separate “what happens near x?” from “what has happened up to x?”

PDF: the local view

The probability density function f(x) describes how densely probability is distributed near x. A higher curve means that nearby intervals receive more probability than intervals of the same width where the curve is lower.

Probability is obtained from area, not from the height at one point:

P(a < X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx

The shaded area—not the height of the curve—is P(a<X≤b).

CDF: the accumulated view

The cumulative distribution function F(x) records all probability at or below x. It starts near zero on the far left and increases toward one as x moves to the right.

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

Unlike a density, a CDF is defined for every random variable—discrete, continuous, or mixed. Every CDF is non-decreasing, right-continuous, and has limiting values zero and one at the two ends of the real line.

How the two views connect

For an absolutely continuous random variable, the CDF is the accumulated area under the PDF from −∞ to x.

F(x) = ∫₋∞ˣ f(t) dt

Where differentiation is valid, we may reverse this relationship:

f(x) = F′(x)

This is why the PDF is the local rate at which the CDF is accumulating. A steep part of the CDF corresponds to a high density; a nearly flat part corresponds to a low density.

Two questions

PDF: “How concentrated is probability around here?” CDF: “How much probability has accumulated up to here?”

The common mistake: f(x) is not P(X=x)

For a continuous random variable, a single point has no width, so the area over that point is zero:

P(X=x) = ∫ₓˣ f(t) dt = 0

The value f(x) may be positive—and may even exceed one—without being a probability. Only areas under the density curve represent probabilities.

One-line takeaway

The PDF describes local density; the CDF accumulates that density into probability.
