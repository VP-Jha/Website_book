Why a Borel σ-algebra is larger than the collection of open sets

Begin with open sets. Then watch complements and countable unions build an entire measurable world around them.

By Dr. Vishwa Prakash Jha25 August 20268 min read

On this page

The mental pictureHow generation worksWhat enters the familyThe key distinction

The phrase “Borel σ-algebra” can sound as though it names one more technical collection of sets. The central idea is much simpler: start with every open set, then keep adding whatever is forced upon us by the rules of a σ-algebra.

The mental picture: a family inside a family

Imagine three nested regions. The smallest highlighted region contains all open subsets of ℝ. Around it lies the Borel σ-algebra, denoted by ℬ(ℝ). The outside region represents the full power set 𝒫(ℝ), containing every subset of ℝ.

Every open set is Borel, but the Borel family also contains many non-open sets.

The diagram expresses containment, not equality:

{open subsets of ℝ} ⊂ ℬ(ℝ) ⊂ 𝒫(ℝ)

How one collection generates another

A σ-algebra must contain the whole space and be closed under complements and countable unions. The collection of open sets does not satisfy these requirements. For example, the complement of an open set is closed and need not be open.

So we take all open sets and add their complements. Next, we add all countable unions of the sets now present. Those new sets have complements too, and the rule forces us to add them. The process continues conceptually until no required operation produces a set outside the collection.

ℬ(ℝ) = σ({O ⊆ ℝ : O is open})

The notation σ(·) means “the smallest σ-algebra containing the given collection.” The word smallest matters: we add every set demanded by the σ-algebra rules, but nothing merely for decoration.

Structural insight

Open sets are the generators. Borel sets are everything that can be built from those generators using complements and countable set operations.

What enters the Borel family?

Closed sets

If F is closed, then Fᶜ is open. Because every open set is in ℬ(ℝ) and a σ-algebra is closed under complements, F also lies in ℬ(ℝ).

Fσ sets

A countable union of closed sets is called an Fσ set. Closed sets are Borel, and a σ-algebra is closed under countable unions, so every Fσ set is Borel.

Gδ sets

A countable intersection of open sets is called a Gδ set. Although the definition of σ-algebra mentions countable unions, closure under countable intersections follows from De Morgan’s laws.

⋂ₙ Oₙ = (⋃ₙ Oₙᶜ)ᶜ

The distinction students should retain

“Generated by open sets” does not mean “contains only open sets.” A recipe can create objects different from its ingredients. Closed intervals, singletons, countable sets, Fσ sets, and Gδ sets are all Borel, although most are not open.

One-line takeaway

The Borel σ-algebra is the smallest collection that contains every open set and remains stable under complements and countable unions.
