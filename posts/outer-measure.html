Outer measure: why we cover a set from the outside

An irregular set may not reveal its length directly. Intervals give us a language for approaching it.

By Dr. Vishwa Prakash Jha20 August 20266 min read

On this page

Length is obvious for an interval. It is less obvious for a scattered or highly irregular subset of ℝ. Outer measure solves this by comparing the set with countable collections of intervals whose lengths are already understood.

The problem: an irregular set has no visible ruler

If E is an interval (a,b), its length is b−a. But if E consists of separated fragments, infinitely many points, or a complicated limiting construction, there may be no single left endpoint and right endpoint that describe its size.

Instead of demanding that E describe itself, we place familiar measuring pieces around it.

Cover the set from the outside

Choose a countable collection of open intervals I₁,I₂,… whose union contains E. Such a collection is a cover of E. Add the lengths of the intervals to obtain the total length of that cover.

E ⊆ ⋃ₙ₌₁∞ Iₙ    and    total cover length = Σₙ₌₁∞ ℓ(Iₙ)

The intervals may include points outside E. That is why the measurement is “outer”.

Different covers produce different totals. A careless cover may be very long; a more precise cover may fit closely around the set.

Why do we take the infimum?

There may be no single cover with the least possible total length. Nevertheless, cover lengths can approach a best lower value. The infimum captures that limiting best value without requiring one cover to attain it.

m*(E) = inf { Σₙ₌₁∞ ℓ(Iₙ) : E ⊆ ⋃ₙ₌₁∞ Iₙ }
Mental picture

Imagine wrapping the set again and again with increasingly economical interval covers. Outer measure is the tightest total length that those wrappings can approach.

The infimum is therefore not an arbitrary technical device. It prevents the answer from depending on which particular cover we happened to choose.

Example: why a singleton has outer measure zero

Let E={x}. For every ε>0, the open interval (x−ε/2,x+ε/2) covers x and has length ε. Therefore m*({x})≤ε for every positive ε.

0 ≤ m*({x}) ≤ ε   for every ε>0   ⟹   m*({x})=0

The point has not disappeared. Rather, it can be covered by intervals of arbitrarily small length.

One-line takeaway

Outer measure assigns size by taking the smallest achievable total length of countable interval covers.
